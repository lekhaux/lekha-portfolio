01 · Problem

Speed is the product.
It's also the attack vector.

UPI processes over 10 billion transactions monthly. Its greatest achievement is also its greatest risk: instant, irreversible payments. Once sent, money is gone.

Fraud doesn't happen because users are naive. It happens because the interface gives them no signal that something is wrong. A ₹50 chai payment and a ₹50,000 phishing collect request look identical in every major UPI app today.

"The UX treats every transaction the same. Risk is invisible at the exact moment users need to see it."

Warnings exist — but they're static banners users have been trained to ignore. The real problem is not awareness. It's the absence of designed hesitation.

👁
Risk is invisible
Backend signals (new contact, unusual amount, suspicious keywords) exist but are never surfaced to the user at decision time.
⚡
One UX for all risk levels
Safe and dangerous transactions share the same interface. There is no adaptation based on context or risk score.
🔔
Warnings create blindness
Repeated generic alerts condition users to dismiss them without reading. The warning itself has become invisible.
02 · Research & Insights

What the data
actually tells us

Auditing Google Pay, PhonePe, and Paytm revealed a consistent pattern: fraud prevention is treated as a backend problem, not a UX problem. The design assumes users are already informed. They're not.

Behavioral research shows that users under time pressure (which mobile payments create by default) rely on pattern recognition, not rational evaluation. The interface needs to interrupt that pattern for high-risk scenarios.

Social proof research (Cialdini) and loss aversion studies both point to the same conclusion: specific, contextual, peer-referenced messaging dramatically outperforms generic authority-based warnings.

03 · Solution

A dynamic UX that
adapts to risk

The core idea: don't show the same interface to every transaction. Build a real-time risk scoring engine, then use the score to modulate how the UX behaves — from completely invisible (low risk) to full interruption with multi-step friction (high risk).

Risk Score → UX Behavior

Low Risk · Score 0–30 Normal Flow
Standard payment interface. No interruption. Preserves speed for trusted transactions.
Medium Risk · Score 31–60 Contextual Warning
Specific warning banner with the exact risk signal highlighted. Soft nudge, not a hard stop.
High Risk · Score 61+ Hard Interrupt
Full-screen interrupt → evidence screen → friction gate → final PIN warning. Four deliberate steps.

05 · Design Decisions

Why these choices,
not the obvious ones

Every design decision was made to solve one thing: getting users to pause long enough to process risk. That required deliberate choices that go against the typical "friction is bad" product assumption.

Decline as the primary CTA. Most apps put equal weight on both options. Here, the system nudges users toward safety by making decline large, prominent, and first. Proceeding requires intentional effort.

Specific signals over generic warnings. "This looks risky" is useless. "First-time contact + KYC refund keyword + 6× your usual amount" is credible. Specificity creates belief.

Social proof as evidence. "82% of users declined similar requests" is more persuasive than any institutional warning for mobile-first demographics. It references peers, not authority.

🛑
Hard Interrupt Before CTA
The full-screen interrupt fires before users see the Approve button — breaking the momentum of autopilot behavior.
🔒
Progressive Friction, Not a Wall
Four steps (alert → evidence → gate → PIN warning) feel proportionate. A single hard wall would frustrate genuine users more than deter fraud.
👥
Loss Framing + Social Proof
Combining peer behavior data with concrete loss messaging activates both social norms and loss aversion — two of the most reliable behavioral levers.
06 · Before vs. After

What actually changed

Current State
  • Identical UX for all transactions, regardless of risk
  • Generic "be careful" banners that users dismiss reflexively
  • Single-tap approval — zero friction on dangerous payments
  • Risk signals exist in backend, never shown to user
  • Decline and approve carry equal visual weight
  • No behavioral design — only passive information display
Proposed System
  • Three-tier adaptive UX that scales intervention to risk level
  • Specific, evidence-based alerts citing exact detected signals
  • Four-step friction for high-risk: interrupt → evidence → gate → reminder
  • Risk signals surfaced visually at the moment of decision
  • Decline is primary CTA — system actively nudges safe behavior
  • Six behavioral techniques embedded across the flow

Honest about what this costs

Trade-offs

Risk
False positives will frustrate legitimate users
Rule-based scoring will occasionally interrupt genuine payments. Mitigation: user feedback loop ("This is a trusted merchant"), conservative initial thresholds, and model tuning over time.
Risk
Repeated false positives erode trust in the system
If users are interrupted on legitimate payments too often, they'll start dismissing high-risk alerts just as they do generic ones. Precision matters more than recall here.
Scope
Real-time scoring at scale is an infrastructure investment
This is an engineering constraint the design must account for. Phase 1 can run on rule-based scoring. ML-personalization comes in Phase 2 once signal data matures.
Conclusion

Fraud is a decision design problem — not a backend one.

The signals to detect fraud already exist. The gap is the last three seconds — between a user seeing a request and tapping approve. This system closes that gap by making risk visible, decisions deliberate, and the safe path effortless.

The technology is a means. The outcome is a user who pauses, reads, and chooses — instead of tapping past a warning they've been trained to ignore.
